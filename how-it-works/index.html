How the flying Tesla Roadster works

On {{spec:revealDate}}, at {{spec:revealVenue}}, a roadster drove up a ramp that curled over until it was upside down, clung there, dropped off the end, rolled half a turn in the air and landed on its wheels. Here is how two very different tricks make that possible: underbody fans that suck the car onto the road, and compressed-air thrusters that push it without using the tires at all.

1. The grip ceiling

A tire can only push on the road as hard as the road pushes back on it, times a friction coefficient μ. With nothing else helping, that caps acceleration at μ × g. Lars Moravy said this car's tires manage about {{spec:tireOnlyGrip}} on their own.

F_tire ≤ μ · N     on flat ground N = m · g, so a_max = μ · g

Past that limit, more motor power just spins the wheels. On tires alone our model needs {{calc:tiresOnly060}} to reach 60 mph however much power you give it. There are two ways out: press the car harder onto the road without adding mass, or push the car directly instead of pushing the road.

2. Sucked to the road

Fans under the floor pump air out from beneath the car, and deployable skirts close the gap to the road (patent {{spec:fanPatent}}). The air pressure on top of the car is now higher than underneath, so the atmosphere presses the whole car down, even at a standstill. On the reveal ramp the downforce readout showed {{spec:invertedDownforce}}.

Downforce D = Δp × A_floor × seal efficiency

The surprising part: that readout is a force of about {{calc:downforceKN}}. Spread over a sealed floor of about {{spec:floorArea}}, it needs a pressure drop of only {{spec:suctionTarget}}, which is {{calc:suctionPercent}} below ambient. That is roughly the pressure difference between sea level and a hilltop {{calc:hillAltitude}} up. The trick is the big area, not a strong vacuum.

The catch is the seal. Air leaks back in under the skirts, and the fans have to pump it out again as fast as it comes in. Leakage grows quickly with the gap, so once the seal breaks, suction collapses within a fraction of a second. That is what makes the car drop away cleanly at the end of the curl.

Because the force follows the car's own floor, it works on walls and ceilings too. Upside down, the tires are still pressed against the surface with the suction minus the car's weight, about {{calc:ceilingLoadKN}}. Reported cornering with the fans: {{spec:fanCorneringG}}. The fans run on the battery: {{spec:fanEnergy}}. Fan cars are not new; see the Brabham BT46B and the McMurtry Spéirling in the sources.

3. A tank of air

The thrusters burn nothing. They release air (mostly nitrogen) stored in carbon composite-overwrapped pressure vessels (COPVs). The Thrust trim has {{spec:thrustTankVolume}} behind the seats. The Rocket adds {{spec:rocketSecondTank}} where the passenger seat would be, for {{spec:airVolumeTotal}} holding {{spec:airMass}}. An in-car screen showed {{spec:thrusterPressure}}; older Musk statements said {{spec:muskPressure}}.

An ideal gas at that pressure would hold {{calc:idealGasMass}} in those tanks, more than reported. Real gas this dense is less compressible, so we use a compressibility factor Z of {{spec:compressibility}}, calibrated to the reported mass.

No pump refills the tank while the car moves. The gas left inside does work pushing the rest out, so it cools, and the pressure falls even faster than the mass does:

adiabatic: T = T₀ · (p / p₀)^((γ − 1) / γ), with γ = {{spec:gammaAir}}

4. How a nozzle makes thrust

Each thruster is a valve plus a converging–diverging nozzle. In the narrowest part, the throat, the air reaches the speed of sound and the mass flow is "choked": it depends only on tank pressure and temperature. The widening bell after the throat accelerates the gas to about Mach {{calc:exitMach}}. It leaves at an effective {{calc:exhaustVelocity}}, still above ambient pressure, so it keeps expanding outside the nozzle in a chain of shock diamonds.

ṁ = C_d · A_t · p₀ · √(γ / (R·T₀)) · (2 / (γ + 1))^((γ + 1) / (2(γ − 1)))
F = ṁ · v_e + (p_e − p_a) · A_e

The four rear thrusters in the diffuser together make {{spec:rearThrustTotal}}. Air thrust doesn't go through the tires, so it simply adds to whatever the tires can do. Because the valves are either fully open or shut, partial thrust comes from pulsing them (pulse-width modulation) with a period of {{spec:pwmPeriod}}.

5. The hop and the flight computer

The Rocket adds six more nozzles. By MotorTrend's analysis, {{spec:downNozzleCount}} point down for lift and {{spec:yawNozzleCount}} at the nose point left and right for yaw. Fired together, the down nozzles lift the car; fired unevenly, they pitch and roll it. {{spec:flightActuators}}.

Hovering a {{spec:vehicleMass}} car takes about {{calc:hoverThrust}}, which burns roughly {{calc:hoverFlow}} of air. That is why it hops instead of flying. We sized the lift nozzles at {{spec:downNozzleThrust}} each, so four of them give the reported {{spec:landingG}} landing readout.

6. The curl, step by step

  1. Up the wall. Suction plus the centripetal load from the curve press the tires on, so the car can drive up the curve and onto the ceiling at modest speed.
  2. Upside down. On the ceiling, the tires carry the suction minus the car's own weight, so the car hangs on with grip to spare.
  3. The lip. As the floor leaves the end of the ramp, the skirts lose their seal and suction collapses within a fraction of a second. The car drops.
  4. The roll. The flight computer fires the lift nozzles on one side. A car spins far more easily about its long axis than end over end, so half a turn takes well under a second.
  5. The landing burn. The car falls freely, then brakes hard with all four lift nozzles just before touchdown. Waiting as long as possible saves air. In our simulation the autopilot does this using a bit under half the tank.

7. The limits

A full fill buys {{spec:cellcogFullThrust}} of full thrust (our model: {{calc:fullThrustSeconds}}). Refilling from an external nitrogen supply currently takes {{spec:refillHoursMin}} to {{spec:refillHoursMax}}. {{spec:selfRefill}}. Just compressing that much air takes at least {{energy:ideal}} in theory, and around {{energy:real}} with a real compressor:

W_min = m · R · T · ln(p_tank / p_ambient)

The Rocket is {{spec:rocketAccess}}. Its claimed acceleration works out to {{spec:rocketAccelG}}, which is hard on people too.

8. The three trims

The Performance trim has fans and active aero but no thrusters. Thrust adds the 4 rear thrusters. Rocket adds the full array and a second tank. Both thruster trims reportedly have the same four rear thrusters, yet Thrust is claimed to be much slower, and Tesla hasn't said why. Part of the gap is real: the smaller tank loses pressure faster. To match the claim we also cap the Thrust trim's average valve duty at {{spec:thrustTrimLaunchDuty}}. The demo run was timed at {{spec:demoZeroToSixty}}. Top speed target: {{spec:topSpeed}}.
